AI has been transformative in so many areas, not least software engineering. But we risk losing the skills that make us engineers

---

## The problem
A couple of years ago I wrote a blog on what I called 'low level learning', and while AI did exist at the time it was nowhere near as prevalent as today and I also made a point of never using it in line with my philosophy as not to hinder my learning while at uni. While that blog didn't explicitly call out AI, it did hint at a very big problem I forsaw with it, what I call 'cognitive outsourcing' which then ultimately leads to the now well documented phenomenom of 'cognnitive debt'. Effectively using AI to code and problem solve erodes your ability to think critically and if you aren't already a subject matter expert hamstrings the learning process without very careful usage to introduce more friction to the learning process. We learn by cognative load after all. Obviously this presents a big problem to the junior engineer of today: how on earth do we learn to be a software engineer, and not simply be a codex manager, while keeping pace with post AI delivery throughput expectations? And how do we ensure we aren't contributing to the quality degredation we are seeing in software more widely?

## The upsides 
Before we move onto the solution and my own process it's good to talk about the positives, AI isn't all doom and gloom, far from it. In the right hands, for the right use cases, AI can be an exceptional productivity enhancer however particuarly in engineering fields it will always be best in the hands on an expert. Experts can use their knowledge to spec out the problem, architect the software and have prototypes ready within minutes for further refinement and eventual review, the review being critical here to maintain quality and understanding of the codebase. What we will see with this workload is everyone writing much less code, but spending more time on properly defining the problem and on quality checking the outputs of the AI tools with the net effect being much faster delivery without compromising quality. AI is also fantastic at statically analysing code for security and quality issues, and while this makes cyber defence much more expensive as now software is only as secure as the number of tokens you've thrown at hardening it, the end result will be higher quality and more secure code. Good for all of us in the long term. And of course finally, I think everyone should be using it to automate the tedious, cognitively easy parts of their job such as writing documentation, as long as the prose is good, the content accurate and document reviewed AI can help minimise the boring tasks we all hate doing.

## The solution
Back to the solution. Now from my perspective the key for a junior engineer is to absolutely never comprimise on the problem solving and design phase, even if there's a throughout pressure you always need to think about the architecture of what you're doing. Not only does this keep a check on quality but also ensures you keep developing the skill of being an engineer. Further to this, whereever possible, don't use AI for the implementation phase either because to be a senior engineer you need to know how to do the implementatioh phase, so that the review stage can be done properly and end quality of the code maintained. It also prevents cogniive debt slipping in, we're not at the point yet where humans can have no knowledge of the specifics of the codebase. Finally the most important point, especially when using AI to implement code is to not just ship it without self review and then just expecting the senior reviewer to point out all the flaws, that you will then feed back into the LLM for fixes. This is just bad etiquette and will just lead to slower throughput, eliminating the advantages of AI regardless. You should be using AI for though is to look for bugs and obvious issues in your code before review, this can elevate the quality of delivery significantly.


## What I plan on doing
Draft: I've found AI has helped me get to a minimum required knowledge for my current work while using it in a way that explains everything, but after that I should pull back and do it the hard way

So after using AI a reasonable amount to learn how best to use it in a professional setting I have decided to pull back my usage and focus much more on becoming a good engineer at this stage in my career. There will be a renewed focus of the architecture that goes into my code and especially on the implementation phase, even more so with unfamiliar languages. I have that AI has allowed me to gain the required knowledge to 'do the job' in new areas for me, first in platform engineering and then later in compilers and I attribute this to always having it explain what it's doing, justifying decisions and explaining unfamilar cocnepts to me but after getting to this minimum level I now feel its important to pull back and learn the hard way, from first principles, to become an expert and once that level has been achieved AI will once again become exceptionally useful.

Like all new technologies the advantages and disadvantages are not yet clear with AI, but we know enough now to get the best out of it.
